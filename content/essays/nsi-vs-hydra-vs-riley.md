TL;DR: Hydra is ~~crap~~ a bad choice if you need to send data from a digital content creation app (DCC, e.g. Maya, Blender) to a renderer.

_An architectural review for people who write [offline renderers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offline_rendering), write exporters, or have to live with the result. It compares Hydra and [NSI](https://nsi.readthedocs.io) in detail, then uses Pixar's Riley as a control: it was built for the same job as NSI._

_This is not an adoption survey, and market share or who signs whose cheques are out of scope. It is about the shape of the two designs and what that shape costs._

---

## What

**Hydra** is the rendering framework inside OpenUSD. It started life as the viewport for Pixar's Presto and for `usdview`, with the Storm rasterizer as its first and for years only back end ([Pixar's own account](https://openusd.org/release/intro.html)). Offline renderers were retrofitted later as "render delegates". Its public surface is a set of C++17 class hierarchies (`HdRenderDelegate`, `HdSceneDelegate`, `HdSceneIndexBase`, `HdRprim`, `HdSprim`, `HdBprim`, `HdRenderPass`, `HdRenderBuffer`, ...) living inside the `pxr` libraries. Everything Hydra manages is a "prim", in three flavours you will meet below: Rprims are renderable prims, i.e. geometry; Sprims are state prims, the non-drawable things that carry state, i.e. cameras, lights, materials, coordinate systems; Bprims are buffer prims, i.e. render buffers, render settings and volume field files. It is designed by the OpenUSD working groups and evolved quarterly.

Whether Pixar considered NSI and passed it over comes up again in the Riley section, including a first-hand account. It was not NIH. The actual reason is more interesting.

**NSI**, the Nodal Scene Interface, is 3Delight's API. Olivier Paquet, Aghiles Kheffache (3Delight), and Berj Bannayan (Soho VFX) designed after about twenty years of writing RenderMan-style DCC exporters. They knew where that hurts. The spec is a [64-page PDF](https://s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/3delight-downloads/nsi/nsi.pdf) and a [readthedocs mirror](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/) (the latter also has a few newer node drafts, marked as such, which no renderer implements yet).

Its surface is eleven C functions and roughly two dozen node types.

**Riley** has been RenderMan's own scene API since RenderMan 22 in 2018. It is what Pixar's bridges and _hdPrman_ actually feed to the renderer. It matters because it was designed for the same job as NSI, at about the same time: a renderer-owned push API for DCC bridges. That makes it the control group. It gets its own section after the two main APIs.

The two APIs started from different problems. Keep that in mind throughout.

> Hydra's founding problem: _draw a USD stage in a viewport, fast, and re-draw only what changed._

> NSI's founding problem: _get a scene out of a DCC and into an offline renderer, live edits included, in a way that survives a decade of DCC and renderer releases._

Most differences below follow from those two sentences. Where I am hard on Hydra, it is for what it was later asked to become, not for what it was originally built to do.

This is not a two-to-one difference. The renderer-facing surface is closer to _two orders of magnitude_ apart between NSI and Hydra.

A big surface is not automatically bad. A viewport framework has more jobs than a scene API does, and some of that size is work you would otherwise write yourself.

But every virtual, token and schema is a versioned contract. Hydra has thousands. Somebody pays for that; most of this post is about who.

## Who

The difference between those APIs is data flow. Who owns the scene?

**Hydra is pull.** In Hydra 1.0, the renderer's prims receive `Sync()` with a dirty-bit mask, then call back into `HdSceneDelegate`: `GetMeshTopology`, `Get(id, "points")`, `GetPrimvarDescriptors`, `SampleTransform`, `GetMaterialResource` and about forty more. Hydra 2.0 does the same through a lazily evaluated tree of `HdDataSourceBase` objects behind schema facades. Either way, the DCC retains the source of truth. Hydra has an intermediate copy in the render index and the renderer has another in its own scene: at least three copies of the scene. [`UsdImagingDelegate`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/pxr/usdImaging/usdImaging/delegate.h) adds resolved-attribute caches on top.

Pull is the right design for a viewport. A rasterizer redraws every frame. It does not want to own gigabytes of vertex data it may not draw. A fine-grained "only these bits changed" signal is what it needs.

It is a dubious design for an offline renderer. That one must own the scene to build acceleration structures over it. And that it has to be told, not asked, when a batch of edits is finished.

There is no "the scene is about to change" and no "the scene has finished changing" call in Hydra. The renderer learns about edits by having its `Sync()` methods called, in parallel, from a TBB pool, in an order it does not control.

[OpenUSD issue #1660](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/issues/1660) (from 2021, still open) asks for exactly such a callback because "the first Sync() call ... occurs after substantial delta processing has already completed, and executes simultaneously from multiple threads". [`HdRenderDelegate::CommitResources()`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/pxr/imaging/hd/renderDelegate.h) is the single serialization point. So every offline delegate builds its own edit-batching machinery around it. hdPrman's [`AcquireRiley()`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/tree/dev/third_party/renderman/plugin/hdPrman) stops the render before every edit. HydraNSI [flips an atomic flag](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/renderParam.h) in every `Sync` and issues one NSI `synchronize` [from the render pass](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/renderPass.cpp).

**NSI is push.** The client calls `NSICreate`, `NSISetAttribute` and `NSIConnect`. The renderer receives the data, copies what it needs and owns its scene from then on. The [spec says explicitly](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/background.html) that it wants _"mostly unidirectional dataflow from the client application to the renderer"_, and that commands are self-contained rather than dependent on current state. There is no read-back. That is deliberate.

NSI has [`NSIRenderControl(action="synchronize")`](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/c-api.html).

Buffer your edits, then call it. The spec says what it means. Interactive renders are progressive by definition. `suspend`, `resume`, `stop` and `wait` do what they say on the tin. A callback tells you whether the render completed, aborted, synchronized or restarted.

That is all of it.

**Riley is push, like NSI.** [The Doxygen](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classRiley.html) splits the class in two. "Rendering Process APIs" are `Render`, `Stop`, `RequestUpdate` and settings. "Scene Manipulation APIs" are everything else, and ["can only be called during the editing phase (i.e. when Render() is not running)."](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classRiley.html) `Render()` blocks until you call `Stop()`, which blocks, or `RequestUpdate()`, which does not and returns ["at some later time when the renderer is ready to synchronize scene edits."](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classRiley.html)

Riley has the same protocol shape as NSI, except NSI gives it a name and Riley leaves it implicit. Riley has no begin-edit or end-edit. An edit block is the gap between `Render()` returning and the next `Render()` call. `EditBegin`/`EditEnd` live one level up in the [Scene Graph API](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classScene.html). Its [`ScopedEdit`](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/structScene_1_1ScopedEdit.html) helper is described as a safer way to define an edit block because it cannot deadlock. The raw calls can.

Two independent clients then found the same race. hdPrman's [`AcquireRiley()`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/third_party/renderman/plugin/hdPrman/renderParam.cpp#L4483) loops on `Stop()` because ["It's possible that IsRendering() is true because we're in the preamble of the render loop, before calling into riley. In that case, Stop() is a no-op"](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/third_party/renderman/plugin/hdPrman/renderParam.cpp#L3606) and ["We've occassionally seen cases where Stop() returns successfully, but the riley threadpools don't shut down right away."](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/third_party/renderman/plugin/hdPrman/renderParam.cpp#L3610) Gaffer's [`Globals.cpp`](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/src/IECoreRenderMan/Globals.cpp#L213) has the identical workaround with the identical explanation: "we call Stop() in the wait loop rather than outside it, so that we get a second chance."

`Modify*` is half-and-half. A `nullptr` argument means leave it alone; a passed parameter list is a full replacement, "as it will completely overwrite the existing set of attributes." Six of the `Modify` calls can return `k_ResendPrimVars`, upon which "the caller should immediately reissue the ModifyGeometryPrototype() call, and include the full primvars for the prototype." Gaffer implements that literally for volumes and calls it, in the header, ["Subclass of Object used to work around oddities of the Riley API."](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/src/IECoreRenderMan/Volume.h#L46)

Riley is write-only, which Gaffer [documents](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/src/IECoreRenderMan/Session.h#L57) because Pixar did not. And ["RenderMan only supports one renderer instance at a time"](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/src/IECoreRenderMan/Session.h#L75), so Gaffer [throws on a second session](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/src/IECoreRenderMan/Session.cpp#L151). NSI's `NSIBegin` returns a context handle; you can have as many as you like.

People usually frame Hydra as interactive and NSI as batch. That gets it backwards.

NSI's [second stated design goal](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/background.html), in the spec's own words: "Interactive Rendering and Scene Edits: a scene description is a series of edits and vice versa."

A batch render is one large edit followed by `start`. A viewport is a long-lived context taking small edits followed by `synchronize`.
The renderer re-tracing only what those edits invalidated, progressive buckets streaming back through an in-process display driver.

Same eleven functions, same node graph, no mode switch between a scene description for a final render or a viewport update during a live session inside a DCC.

All three APIs put change detection on the DCC side.

- Hydra's scene delegate or scene index has to mark dirty bits or emit locator notices.
- NSI's exporter has to know which attributes a change touches.

The difference is what happens next. Hydra routes the result through a framework-owned copy of the scene before the renderer may pull it.

For a rasterizer that redraws every frame, this indirection _is_ the product. For a path tracer that must own the scene anyway, it is another copy, a thread pool and an unordered sync. And one that it never asked for btw.

## How

Both APIs need a vocabulary for geometry, transforms, materials/lights, cameras and instancing.

The philosophies differ as much as the data flow does.

### Geometry

NSI: `mesh`, `plane`, `curves`, `particles`, `procedural`, `volume`, `vdbparticles`, `faceset`, `environment`. Subdivision is a `subdivision.scheme` attribute on [`mesh`](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/nodes/mesh.html) (Catmull-Clark only) with crease and corner arrays. [Curves](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/nodes/curves.html) have a `basis` attribute (linear, b-spline, Catmull-Rom, Hobby). Primvars are attributes with a type, an array length, a count and flags; interpolation class is inferred from count (per face, per vertex) and face-varying data uses a `.indices` companion array.

Hydra ([`tokens.h`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/pxr/imaging/hd/tokens.h)): `mesh`, `basisCurves`, `nurbsCurves`, `nurbsPatch`, `points`, `volume`, `tetMesh`, `particleField`, `geomSubset`, `model`, and the implicit shapes `capsule`, `cone`, `cube`, `cylinder`, `sphere`, `plane`. Subdivision has four schemes and full `HdSubdivTags` (interpolation rules for boundaries and face-varying data). Curves have a type, a basis (including Bezier and centripetal Catmull-Rom) and a wrap mode. Primvars carry an explicit interpolation token (`constant`, `uniform`, `varying`, `vertex`, `faceVarying`, `instance`) and a role.

Riley: `CreateGeometryPrototype(userId, RtUString type, displacementId, RtPrimVarList primvars)`. The docs call `type` ["The type of geometry to create"](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classRiley.html) and leave it there; they never document the vocabulary. You have to recover it from `RixStr` in hdPrman and Gaffer, and from the Scene Graph API: `Ri:PolygonMesh`, `Ri:SubdivisionMesh`, `Ri:Curves`, `Ri:Points`, `Ri:Volume`, `Ri:Group`, the [quadrics](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classQuadric.html) `Ri:Sphere`, `Ri:Cone`, `Ri:Cylinder`, `Ri:Hyperboloid`, `Ri:Paraboloid`, `Ri:Disk`, `Ri:Torus`, and presumably `Ri:NuPatch`, `Ri:Blobby` and `Ri:Procedural`. It is the 1989 RenderMan primitive list with its original names.

The encodings are 1989 too. Subdivision [scheme](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classMesh.html) is a primvar, `Ri:scheme`, one of `none`, `catmull-clark`, `loop`, `bilinear`. Topology is two primvars. Creases, corners and boundary rules are five parallel flat arrays, `Ri:subdivtags`, `Ri:subdivtagnargs` (three ints per tag), `Ri:subdivtagintargs`, `Ri:subdivtagfloatargs`, `Ri:subdivtagstringtags`, i.e. `RiHierarchicalSubdivisionMesh`'s argument list stuffed into a param list. hdPrman and Gaffer [do not even agree](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/src/IECoreRenderMan/MeshAlgo.cpp) on how to encode `interpolateboundary` in it. Curves take `Ri:type`, `Ri:Basis`, `Ri:wrap`, and the [varying-count tables per basis](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classCurves.html) are published in the docs and validated by nobody; hdPrman recomputes them by hand.

Primvars are the good part. `RtPrimVarList` carries explicit detail, `constant`, `uniform`, `vertex`, `varying`, `facevarying`, set per parameter. Explicit, like Hydra. Better than NSI. Then the bad part: time samples are a property of the whole list, and only `P` may be animated. Gaffer's [`GeometryAlgo.cpp`](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/src/IECoreRenderMan/GeometryAlgo.cpp#L412): "P is the only primitive variable that RenderMan allows to be animated ... It seems we need to call this before adding any primitive variables, animated or not, or we get crashes in RenderMan." NSI puts time on any attribute, in any order.

Hydra's geometry vocabulary is bigger, and on primvars it and Riley are both better specified than NSI. Explicit interpolation tokens beat count-inference, no argument.

Bigger is a different question, and I will get back to it when NSI gets its turn. Preview: VFX settled on polygons and Catmull-Clark a long time ago, and most of the extra entries in that list are the 1989 RenderMan primitive zoo, inherited via UsdGeom.

Two more caveats. The implicit shapes are not something a delegate must implement: [`HdsiImplicitSurfaceSceneIndex`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/pxr/imaging/hdsi/implicitSurfaceSceneIndex.h) tessellates them into meshes if you do not claim them. And the richness is USD's richness. The prim types, the primvar names (`points`, `normals`, `widths`, `displayColor`, `velocities`, `accelerations`), the interpolation tokens, the subdivision tags -- UsdGeom's, verbatim.

Which is fine when your scene comes from USD. Hoewver, it is a large import of somebody else's ontology when it does not.

### Lights

NSI has no light nodes because it is built around the idea that shading is Open Shading Language (OSL) territory.

Or to quote [the spec](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/background.html): "there are no explicit light sources in NSI: light sources are created by connecting shaders with an emission() closure to a geometry." A rect light is a `mesh` with an emissive shader. An environment is an `environment` node with a shader.

That falls out of the OSL closure model. Every light is geometry, so it gets the visibility, linking and instancing that geometry already has. For free.

Hydra has nine light Sprim types mirroring UsdLux (`rectLight`, `sphereLight`, `diskLight`, `cylinderLight`, `distantLight`, `domeLight`, `meshLight`, `pluginLight`, `lightFilter`) with parameters named after UsdLux attributes. Pixar's own ["UsdLux for renderers" whitepaper](https://openusd.org/release/wp_usdlux_for_renderers.html) concedes that "every renderer has its own 'signature' for each of the core light types that will not perfectly match the UsdLux schemas, thus necessitating a remapping" which is "largely hard-coded in Hydra render delegates", and that the Hydra light tokens "must be kept in sync with any changes to UsdLux". So HydraNSI reimplements `UsdLuxLight::ComputeBaseEmission()` by hand. [The comment](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/light.cpp#L428) explains why: "I don't have access to USD scene to build a UsdLuxLight".

Riley: `CreateLightShader(userId, lightNetwork, lightFilterNetwork)` and `CreateLightInstance(userId, groupPrototype, geoPrototype, material, lightShader, coordsys, transform, attributes)`. The second signature is `CreateGeometryInstance` with one extra id. A light is a geometry instance with a light shader. That is NSI's model too, and it is the right one.

Except the geometry is optional, and in practice absent. For every analytic light, both hdPrman and Gaffer pass `GeometryPrototypeId::InvalidId()`.

[The doc's](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classRiley.html) "This must be a handle returned from CreateGeometryPrototype()" is contradicted by the only two clients in existence. So light shape is _not_ geometry.

hdPrman [bakes the rect light's width and height into the instance transform's scale](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/third_party/renderman/plugin/hdPrman/light.cpp), and [rotates the dome](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/third_party/renderman/plugin/hdPrman/light.cpp#L1059) by -90 X and 90 Y to "match OpenEXR spec for environment maps." In NSI the rect is geometry.

Light filters, per Gaffer's [`LightLinker.h`](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/src/IECoreRenderMan/LightLinker.h#L57): "Light filters aren't first-class objects in Riley. Instead they are just extra shaders bolted on to the shader owned by the light." One filter network per light, so [every host](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/include/IECoreRenderMan/ShaderNetworkAlgo.h#L54) inserts a `PxrCombinerLightFilter` to hold more than one. Light linking is the RIB-era string-set match: [`grouping:membership`](https://renderman.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/REN26/pages/19661983/Instance+Attributes) on the light, `lighting:subset` on the geometry, space-separated names. Gaffer's [comment](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/src/IECoreRenderMan/LightLinker.cpp#L198) on the cost: "We are updating all lights any time linking changes." NSI's linking is a connection between two nodes.

### Materials

NSI: a `shader` node points at an `.oso` file (or inline compiled OSL, or a MaterialX node definition). Shader parameters are attributes. Shader-to-shader connections are `NSIConnect`. The shading network's output connects to `surfaceshader`, `displacementshader` or `volumeshader` on an `attributes` node, which connects to geometry. Primvars are read from inside the shader with `getattribute()`. There is [no symbolic primvar binding](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/nodes/shader.html) in the API.

Hydra: [`HdMaterialNetworkMap`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/pxr/imaging/hd/material.h), keyed by a render-context token (`universalRenderContext`, `ri`, `arnold`, `mtlx`), containing `HdMaterialNetwork2` nodes identified by Sdr shader identifiers with typed parameters and connections. In practice the identifiers a host will send you are `UsdPreviewSurface`, `UsdUVTexture`, `UsdPrimvarReader_*`, or MaterialX node definitions, unless the host has a hook for forwarding vendor-specific shader nodes and the vendor has registered them with Sdr. This has changed names once (`HdMaterialNetwork` to `HdMaterialNetwork2`) and grown a third interface (`HdMaterialNetworkInterface`) since.

Riley: `CreateMaterial(userId, ShadingNetwork bxdf, attributes)`. A [`ShadingNetwork`](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classRiley.html) is a flat array of `ShadingNode { type, name, handle, params }`, twelve [node types](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/structShadingNode.html) from `k_Pattern` and `k_Bxdf` through `k_VolumeFilter`, with connections living inside `params` as typed references. Displacement is a separate [`CreateDisplacement`](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classRiley.html), and its id is bound to the _prototype_, not the instance, so hdPrman notes "Displacement networks are passed to the geom prototype; material networks are passed to the instances."

OSL is native but only for patterns. [Since RenderMan 24](https://renderman.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/REN24/pages/21759276), "all patterns are OSL shaders", per hdPrman's [`material.cpp`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/third_party/renderman/plugin/hdPrman/material.cpp#L359), and a Bxdf or Pattern node's `name` is a resolved `.oso` path. Only the Bxdf remains C++ under RIS. That Pixar went from RenderMan Shading language over the mess to that was class-based shaders (which 3Delight copied, not meast on my youtful and ignorant request at time) is mind-boggling and deserves a rant, er post on its own.

However, there is light as under XPU Bxdfs are OSL too, again. Of the three APIs, Riley is closest to NSI's shading model: opaque shader nodes, OSL, connections, then let the renderer deal with it. It expresses that in C++ structs rather than attribute calls.

The philosophies: NSI and Riley treat shading as data the renderer understands, i.e. OSL, and the API as a pipe. Hydra treats shading as a typed graph the _framework_ understands.

Again, that is the right call for a viewport. You cannot generate GLSL from an opaque `.oso`. An offline renderer with its own shading language mostly translates it away again. And again: Hydra is advertised as a way to talk to an offline renderer, not a viewport. By now you should get a feeling for why this is misleading at best.

### Attributes, Inheritance & Scene Graph

NSI has an [`attributes` node](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/nodes/attributes.html) that connects to geometry or transforms and carries visibility flags, matte, shaders, and user attributes. Attributes are gathered up the DAG toward `.root`; conflicts are resolved by an explicit priority and then by proximity. Light linking and inter-object visibility are cross-hierarchy `NSIConnect` calls with a `value` argument. A `set` node groups anything. Instancing is the DAG: connect one geometry to several transforms and you have instances; connect transforms to transforms and you have instances of instances; the [`instances` node](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/nodes/instances.html) handles the bulk case with matrices and model indices.

Hydra's scene index flattens. [`HdFlatteningSceneIndex`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/pxr/imaging/hd/flatteningSceneIndex.h) resolves transforms, visibility, purpose and material bindings down to each prim before the renderer sees them (and [since USD 25.11](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/CHANGELOG.md#2511---2025-10-24) the scene-index path is on by default even for legacy delegates). Light linking arrives as `categories` tokens per prim, resolved by [`HdsiLightLinkingSceneIndex`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/pxr/imaging/hdsi/lightLinkingSceneIndex.h#L73), which still documents "Linking to instance proxy prims, nested instances and nested point instancers is not yet supported". Instancing is `HdInstancer`, a point-cloud-style prototype-and-indices model. Per-face material assignment is `geomSubset` child prims, which Storm itself ignored for years (issues [#542](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/issues/542), [#1896](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/issues/1896)).

Riley is flat. Confirmed, not inferred: there is no hierarchy, no attribute stack, no inheritance. Every `GeometryInstance` carries a complete `RtParamList` of [instance attributes](https://renderman.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/REN26/pages/19661983/Instance+Attributes), and `ModifyGeometryInstance` overwrites the lot. Inheritance is a [Scene Graph API](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classGroup.html) feature, "updating parameters along DAG hierarchies to generate the instance attribute list used for rendering", flattened before Riley sees it. Same as Hydra's flattening scene index, but at the renderer boundary, where nothing downstream can undo it. Of the three, NSI is the only API that keeps the DAG.

Flat sounds simpler. For the renderer it is. For the person (or agent, I guess) writing the exporter it is more work, more surface, more ways stuff can break or drift. And the work never ends, because no DCC scene is flat. Maya, Houdini, Katana and Cinema 4D all hand you a tree of transforms. Against a flat API you compose that tree yourself and hand over leaves.
Fine, once. Then the user moves a group node with two hundred descendants, and now change tracking is your problem: you have to work out which two hundred flattened instances that one edit maps to, recompute their matrices, and re-send every one.
Against NSI you set one `transformationmatrix` on one `transform` node and call `synchronize`; the renderer already knows what hangs under it. That is what 3Delight for Houdini's [transform callback](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3delight-for-houdini/-/blob/master/null.cpp#L133) does, in about thirty lines.

Gaffer's session header spells it out for Riley: "if we want access to any state we need to track it ourselves." Hydra gives the scene-delegate author the same job. A flat API moves the hardest exporter problem -- keeping the renderer's view in sync with a changing hierarchy -- from the API into every client. NSI was shaped by people who had written those clients; Hydra and Riley by people who had written the renderer.

There is one split Riley does have, and it is a trap. [Primitive variables](https://renderman.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/REN26/pages/19662093/Primitive+Variables) "may also be referred to as prototype attributes for clarity versus Instance Attributes which may be varied per instance." So `dice:*`, `trace:bias`, `displacementbound:*`, `Ri:Orientation`, `volume:*`, `curve:*`, `shade:faceset` are primvars on the prototype, not attributes on the instance. Gaffer hard-codes [a 46-entry list](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/src/IECoreRenderMan/Attributes.cpp) generated from `PRManPrimVars.args` to sort them; hdPrman has its own. Get one wrong and it silently does nothing.

Instancing is `GeometryPrototype` plus `GeometryInstance`, with nesting through `groupPrototypeId`: a prototype of type `Ri:Group` that you create instances into. Sound. Now hdPrman's [`instancer.cpp`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/third_party/renderman/plugin/hdPrman/instancer.cpp#L89) on what that costs: `HDPRMAN_MAX_SUPPORTED_NESTING_DEPTH = 4`, beyond which "instances must flatten into their parents"; the light-linking and visibility attributes are "not supported on instances inside geometry prototype groups", so the instancer splits groups by hashing those; ["Prman does not handle lights in prototype groups well"](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/third_party/renderman/plugin/hdPrman/instancer.cpp#L1298); and nesting ["breaks selection past the top-level of instances."](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/third_party/renderman/plugin/hdPrman/instancer.cpp#L1787) Per-instance primvars are [not allowed at all](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classGeometry.html), "may not vary per instance", so both hosts smuggle them in as `user:` attributes. The batch `CreateGeometryInstances` added in 26 "do not have any significant advantages over their non-batched versions", [per Pixar](https://renderman.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/REN26/pages/19661991/API+Changes+from+25.X+to+26.X), and neither client uses them.

NSI's `instances` node takes matrices, model indices and per-instance attributes, nests by being a node in the DAG, and has none of the above caveats. It also does not have Riley's twenty years of production behind it, which is worth saying.

HydraNSI's author on instancing, from [`pointInstancer.h`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/pointInstancer.h#L19): "The way this works in Hydra is a little awkward. The instancers are not first class primitives ... This is likely another kludge inherited from the needs of the GL renderer." And in [`pointInstancer.cpp`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/pointInstancer.cpp#L208): "we attempt to rebuild USD instancing indices from Hydra's scrambled idea of what instancing should be like. Hydra has no way of grouping several pieces of geometry together for instancing. Instead, they will show up as separate prototypes but each have the same instance indices array." The delegate [hashes identical index arrays](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/pointInstancer.cpp#L230) to reconstruct grouping that existed in USD and disappeared in Hydra.

## Output, settings, and everything a viewport does not need

Hydra's viewport origin shows most clearly here. None of this is a bug. It is what happens when a viewport has to file your dailies.

**Output.** Hydra's only output object is [`HdRenderBuffer`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/pxr/imaging/hd/renderBuffer.h): a 2D or 3D image with a format, a `Map()` to CPU memory, and `IsConverged()`. An AOV binding maps a token (`color`, `depth`, `primId`, `Neye`, `primvars:foo`, `lpe:...`) to such a buffer. In core `hd` there is no file name. No output driver. No deep sample. No per-product pixel filter. No cryptomatte. No checkpointing. Houdini's `husk` passes those through as opaque [`delegateRenderProducts`](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/hdk/_h_d_k__u_s_d_hydra.html), a `VtArray<HdAovSettingsMap>`, and a delegate that wants deep output writes it through the renderer's own display drivers, outside Hydra's render buffers.

NSI: `camera.screens -> screen.outputlayers -> outputlayer.outputdrivers -> outputdriver` ([`outputlayer`](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/nodes/outputlayer.html), [`outputdriver`](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/nodes/outputdriver.html)). Layer nodes carry the variable, its source (shader output, attribute, or builtin), scalar format, filter, filter width, alpha, colorprofile, light set, cryptomatte level. Driver nodes name a driver and a file. Many cameras, each with many screens, each with many layers, is the normal case rather than a special one.

Nothing novel about it. It is a cleaned-up version of what every RenderMan exporter has done since the nineties, written by people who had written those exporters.

Riley kept that model intact, and here it is the strongest of the three. [`CreateRenderOutput`](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classRiley.html) takes a name, a type, a source, an accumulation rule from eleven (`filter`, `average`, `min`, `max`, `zmin`, `zmax`, `sum`, `variance`, `mse`, `even`, `odd`), a filter from eleven, a filter size and a relative pixel variance. `CreateRenderTarget` groups outputs with an extent and a filter mode. `CreateDisplay` names a [driver](https://renderman.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/REN26/pages/19661875/Display+Drivers) and a file: `openexr`, `deepexr`, `tiff`, `png`, `it`. Deep is a driver, not a flag. `CreateRenderView` binds target, camera and integrator. This is RIB's `Display` and `DisplayChannel`, grown up, and NSI's `outputlayer` is the same idea with fewer accumulation rules. Hydra has a framebuffer.

Two footnotes on Riley's output. The filter mode `importance` means "all outputs of the render target with a filter accumulation rule will use the filter of the first render output", so Gaffer [refuses per-output filters](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/src/IECoreRenderMan/Globals.cpp#L429) as "completely misleading." And cryptomatte is a [sample filter](https://renderman.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/REN27/pages/542235274/PxrCryptomatte), while `CreateRenderView`'s own docs say the sample filter list is "currently ignored", and XPU uses a display driver for it instead.

**Render settings.** Hydra had, until 2022, only a flat `HdRenderSettingsMap` of key-value pairs and a `GetRenderSettingDescriptors()` that is really a UI export. [OpenUSD issue #910](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/issues/910) (2019, still open) documents that to get shot settings from a file a delegate had to invent "a custom schema type, a custom adapter for USD imaging". The [`HdRenderSettings`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/pxr/imaging/hd/renderSettings.h) Bprim now exists but flows only through the scene-index path and only if the renderer registers a filtering scene index naming its namespaces, or the settings get ["filtered out and discarded"](https://forum.aousd.org/t/rendersettings-in-usdrecord-framerecorder/1912). Houdini meanwhile sends [its own conventions](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/hdk/_h_d_k__u_s_d_hydra.html): `houdini:frame`, `houdini:fps`, `renderCameraPath`, `batchCommandLine`, and `huskDelegateOptions`, which is [a JSON string](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/renderDelegate.cpp#L403).

HydraNSI reads settings through three unrelated channels and has, in [`renderParam.h`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/renderParam.h#L88), a six-level priority list for where the camera shutter comes from. [The commit message](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/commit/b2ce6fa) that introduced it: "The scene delegate SampleTransform(), SamplePrimvar(), etc methods now require a shutter range ... There are, of course, half a dozen ways to get that and none which works all the time."

NSI has a [`global`](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/nodes/global.html) node and a [`screen`](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/nodes/screen.html) node.

The spec is honest that most `global` attributes are "implementation specific". A real weakness, and I return to it below. But there is exactly one place to look, and you can set it whenever you like.

Riley has [`SetOptions`](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classRiley.html), one bag, and the docs: "Current Riley implementations require SetOptions() to be the first call made before any other scene manipulation. Additionally, only a small subset of options edits are currently supported." hdPrman [adds](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/third_party/renderman/plugin/hdPrman/renderParam.cpp#L3420) that "Several riley settings are immutable and need to be set on the first SetOptions call." The [vocabulary](https://renderman.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/REN26/pages/19661985/Renderer+Options) is the RIB one, `Ri:FormatResolution`, `Ri:CropWindow`, `Ri:PixelVariance`, `Ri:Shutter`, `bucket:order`, `checkpoint:*`, `hider:*`, and hdPrman [lists four](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/third_party/renderman/plugin/hdPrman/renderParam.cpp) that "should not be given to Riley::SetOptions() anymore." There is one place to look; it must be the first call or Riley can crash, and most of it is read-only afterwards.

Cameras, while we are here: [`CreateCamera`](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classRiley.html) requires globally unique names, and "Attempting to create a camera name that is already in use is undefined behavior." The perspective projection node is `PxrCamera`, not `PxrPerspective`, which both clients had to discover.

**Frames and shutter.** Hydra has no notion of a frame or a frame range; the host sets stage time and re-syncs. Shutter lives on the camera Sprim or in the render settings prim, and motion samples are pulled per prim with `SamplePrimvar(maxSampleCount, ...)`. A silently renamed setting (`instantaneousShutter` to `disableMotionBlur`) [broke third-party motion blur in Houdini 19.5](https://forum.aousd.org/t/motion-blur-for-custom-prim-adapter-stopped-working-in-solaris-for-houdini-19-5/874) because sampling was suddenly "always being invoked with maxNumSamples set to 1". Velocity blur is not core: hdPrman ships a 44 KB [`velocityMotionResolvingSceneIndexPlugin`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/tree/dev/third_party/renderman/plugin/hdPrman); HydraNSI ships a [506-line filtering scene index](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/accelerationBlurPlugin.cpp) to synthesize samples from velocities and accelerations.

Riley: `Ri:Frame` is a [scalar option](https://renderman.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/REN26/pages/19661985/Renderer+Options). No range; one `Render()` is one image. Motion is `Transform { samples, matrices, times }` on instances and cameras, which is fine, and time samples on `P` only, as above. Shutter is defined in four places: the `Ri:Shutter` option, the camera's `shutterOpenTime` and `shutterCloseTime`, the camera's eight-float `shutteropening` curve, and the `Shutter:offset` option. Gaffer [sets the option twice](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/src/IECoreRenderMan/Globals.cpp) because "Riley needs the Ri:Shutter option to be set on creation, but we would normally only pass it along later with the render camera." Not six places like HydraNSI's Hydra. Not one like NSI.

NSI: [`NSISetAttributeAtTime(ctx, handle, time, ...)`](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/c-api.html). Any attribute, any number of samples, any order.

"Any order" is why the call exists in that form. In Ri, motion was [`RiMotionBegin` ... geometry ... `RiMotionEnd`](https://paulbourke.net/dataformats/rib/RISpec3_2.pdf): every time sample of a primitive had to be known when the primitive was declared, because the samples were part of its definition. An exporter therefore had to step the DCC to each shutter sample for each object in turn. In Maya, a particle system or simulation makes jumping time back and forth extra slow because it has to re-evaluate. Anyone who has exported a Maya scene with nParticles to RIB has watched the timeline scrub back and forth, object by object, for minutes. The DCC and the API's requirements were at war, and the artist paid.

That experience went straight into NSI. Because the API is stateless, `NSISetAttributeAtTime` can be called whenever the data is available. Step Maya once to shutter open, emit that sample for every object; step once to shutter close, emit again. Two time changes for the whole scene rather than two per object.

Riley kept the Ri constraint. Time samples are a property of the whole `RtPrimVarList`, handed over at `CreateGeometryPrototype`, and the `Transform` struct carries all its samples at once. Hydra's `SamplePrimvar(maxSampleCount, ...)` is the same shape: the renderer asks for all samples of one prim in one call. Of the three, only NSI lets the exporter visit time in the order the DCC prefers.

Shutter is `shutterrange` and `shutteropening` on the camera.

That is the motion-blur API.

## Change over time, and C vs. C++

If you ship software for money, this section probably decides it.

**NSI is a C ABI.** [Eleven functions](https://github.com/virtualritz/nsi/blob/master/crates/nsi-sys/include/nsi.h), a `struct NSIParam_t`, an enum of types, some flags. The header on my machine is byte-identical between the two 3Delight SDK builds I have installed. The function list has not changed since the 2018 spec.

Consequences:

- A renderer implementing NSI is a shared library with eleven exported symbols. [`nsi-moonray`](https://github.com/virtualritz/nsi-moonray#as-a-drop-in-renderer), the Rust MoonRay back end, ships exactly that: a drop-in `.so` with the NSI C entry points. Every NSI client that loads its renderer dynamically (all the 3Delight DCC plugins do, through [`nsi_dynamic.hpp`](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/nsi_dynamic.hpp.html), and 3Delight for Houdini [does not even link `lib3delight`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3delight-for-houdini/-/blob/master/nsi_loader.cpp)) can in principle be pointed at a different renderer without recompilation.
- Bindings are trivial. The [`nsi` Rust workspace](https://github.com/virtualritz/nsi) wraps the C API in a safe `Context` with the same eleven methods, and builds on top of it a parser, a display-driver framework, an intermediate representation and procedural helpers. Python, Lua and C# bindings are similarly thin. None of them care which compiler, standard library, TBB or Boost the renderer was built with.
- The C++ header, [`nsi.hpp`](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/nsi.hpp.html), is 1145 lines of optional convenience: argument-list helpers and a `NSI::Context` class. It is header-only and can be ignored.

**Hydra is a C++17 class ABI.** Symbols live in versioned inline namespaces (`pxrInternal_v0_YY`). A delegate built against 25.11 will not link against 26.03 whether or not anything actually changed. There is no semver and, [per the AOUSD forum](https://forum.aousd.org/t/decrease-rebuilds-of-plug-ins-between-usd-versions/1643), no written ABI policy ("USD does not follow semver, or any kind of major/minor versioning. It is effectively a rolling release"). Pixar's informal source-compatibility promise is [roughly a year](https://groups.google.com/g/vfx-platform-discuss/c/sB_AhwggvDU/m/zU3wm9MNAQAJ): "USD tends to not introduce any breaking changes within the XY.8 to (XY+1).8 cycle." Every host -- Houdini, Maya, Katana, Blender, Omniverse -- ships its own USD build with its own Python, TBB and Boost. That is why Autodesk, Chaos and 3Delight publish per-host delegate binaries.

The interface version makes the point: [`HD_API_VERSION`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/pxr/imaging/hd/version.h) went from 36 in early 2021 to 105 today, roughly eight to fourteen interface changes a year, accelerating through Hydra 2.0. `HdRenderIndex::New` was renamed at version 100. `HdRendererCreateArgs` was added at 98 and removed at 103, so it lasted about a year. `HdxTaskController` was [deprecated in 26.08](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/CHANGELOG.md#2608---2026-07-20). The 2.0 replacement for `HdRenderDelegate`, [`HdRenderer`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/pxr/imaging/hd/renderer.h#L34), is a stub with one method: `GetLegacyRenderControl()`.

What that costs downstream, measured on HydraNSI: 60 source files, thirty of which carry `#if PXR_VERSION` guards. 79 such lines. 21 distinct version thresholds. A file named [`compatibility.h`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/compatibility.h) exists solely to paper over the removal of one constructor parameter. Of the [last fifty commits](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/commits/master), about fifteen are "Fix build with USD 26.05 and 26.08", "Ndr was removed in 25.05, must use Sdr directly", "Fix Boost.Python linking with Houdini 21", "Fix point instancer" because `GetInstancerPrototypes()` [changed semantics](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/pointInstancer.cpp#L152) somewhere between 24.03 and 25.05.

**Riley is a C++ vtable behind a plugin loader.** `riley::Riley` is a pure-virtual class in a version namespace, `riley::vX_Y`, obtained through [`RixContext::GetRixInterface`](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/RixInterfaces_8h_source.html), and the docs on [linking](https://renderman.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/REN26/pages/19661993/Compiling+Plugins+Linking+Applications): "you must do your due diligence to make sure that the version ... is the version you are expecting ... Otherwise, a crash is likely." Plugins ["need to be built with the same version of the compiler that is specified in the VFX Reference Platform that RenderMan supports."](https://renderman.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/REN26/pages/19661993/Compiling+Plugins+Linking+Applications) No C binding. The Python `rman` module [is the Scene Graph API](https://github.com/prman-pixar/RenderManForBlender/blob/main/rman_render.py), not Riley.

Only one implementation exists, so ABI stability matters only to the clients. It matters to them a lot. The [23 to 25 migration](https://renderman.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/REN25/pages/20417146/Migrating+from+23.X+to+25) removed `Begin()`/`End()`, renamed "Master" to "Prototype" everywhere, removed the `RenderSettings` struct, renamed `SetRenderOptions` and `SetActiveCamera`, replaced the filter-chain calls, split the parameter list class in two, moved the typedefs to a different header and "changed the Id types from enums to classes." [25 to 26](https://renderman.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/REN26/pages/19661991/API+Changes+from+25.X+to+26.X) fixed a const and added the batch calls. [26 to 27](https://renderman.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/REN27/pages/542236989/API+Changes+from+26.X+to+27.X) reports "no significant changes", and a diff of the two headers agrees: purely additive, 0.4 to 0.5.

Riley thrashed for three releases and then settled, yet the header [still says](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/Riley_8h_source.html#l00040) it will "change substantially in the next release." The whole API is 674 non-comment lines. NSI's is 169 and has not moved.

Hydra has no C boundary because it was never meant to have one. Riley has no C boundary because Pixar only meant to call it themselves.

Hydra is the internal architecture of a viewport that was later declared an interface. Riley is the internal architecture of a renderer that was published with a disclaimer. NSI is an interface that was designed as one.

## Serialization and debuggability

Because NSI calls are self-contained data, the API is [its own file format](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/stream-api.html). `NSIBegin` with a `streamfilename` writes every subsequent call as text or binary. `NSIEvaluate` reads it back. A `procedural` node defers it. The 3Delight for Houdini plugin uses this to write a [`.static` sidecar](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3delight-for-houdini/-/blob/master/ROP_3Delight.cpp#L708) of time-independent attributes shared across a frame sequence. HydraNSI uses it to [turn itself into a USD-to-NSI exporter](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/renderDelegate.cpp#L413) with one environment variable. When a render comes out wrong you dump the stream, read it, diff it against yesterday's and edit it by hand. And hand-editing works, because every line is a complete, self-contained call. Delete a line, duplicate one, move one, and the stream still parses. There is no block to close.

Anyone who has debugged a RIB at 2am knows what that is worth.

Hydra has no serialized form. What the renderer received exists only as an object graph inside a host process.

[`HdsiDebuggingSceneIndex`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/pxr/imaging/hdsi/debuggingSceneIndex.h) prints notices. `usdview` has a Hydra Scene Debugger panel. Neither hands you a file you can re-render.

To be fair, the `.usd` input is upstream of all this and can be inspected. Ask for `.usda` and it is text: USD's declarative syntax, not XML. But it is a layer in a composition system, not a call log. The renderer sees only the result after references, sublayers and variants resolve and the result is imaged. You can read the input; you cannot read what the renderer received. And the input is nested block syntax, so a missing curly brace makes the parser throw. Great for hand editing at 2am.

Riley has no serialized form either, and no read-back. What it has is an environment variable, [`RILEY_CAPTURE`](https://renderman.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/REN26/pages/152240132/Environment+Variables), documented in one sentence: "Location of Disgust trace for scenegraph serialization output. Useful for submitting interactive bugs." Format "python or json". It is a call log, not a scene. RenderMan for Blender [writes it as `.debug.py`](https://github.com/prman-pixar/RenderManForBlender/blob/main/rman_render.py); Gaffer [uses the JSON mode in unit tests](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/python/IECoreRenderManTest/RileyCapture.py) to avoid comparing images. Whether the Python is meant to be re-executed, Pixar does not say. RIB export exists, [one layer up](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classScene.html), and RIB is the old interface.

Pixels come back the RIB way, through `ndspy` display drivers registered with [`RixDspy`](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/RixDspy_8h_source.html). hdPrman registers a driver called `hydra` and finds its framebuffer by [passing an integer `bufferID` through the display parameters](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/third_party/renderman/plugin/hdPrman/framebuffer.cpp#L62). Gaffer ships [a display DSO](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/src/IECoreRenderManDisplay/Display.cpp#L60) that passes [the receiving buffer's pointer as a decimal string](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/src/GafferSceneUI/OutputBuffer.cpp), implements two driver APIs side by side, and explains why in a comment: "RenderMan will be dropping this API when XPU becomes the new RenderMan." NSI's display driver ABI is 3Delight's and undocumented in the spec, which I hold against it below. It is also the one the 3DfH viewport, HydraNSI and akatela's Rust display crate all target without incident.

Progress and convergence: Riley has none. There is a separate [`RixEventCallbacks`](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classRixEventCallbacks.html) interface, itself marked "preview release", with a 0 to 100 progress integer. hdPrman sets `_converged = true` when `Render()` returns. NSI has `stoppedcallback` and `progresscallback` as `RenderControl` arguments.

## The third API: Riley, and a note on the word RenderMan

First the word, because it has quietly changed meaning and the change matters for everything below.

A bit over a decade ago "RenderMan" meant the [RenderMan Interface Specification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RenderMan_Interface_Specification). An open spec, 1988, revised 1989, 2000 and 2005, that several renderers implemented: Pixar's PhotoRealistic RenderMan, which everyone called PRMan to tell it apart from the spec, plus 3Delight, BMRT, Air, Aqsis, Pixie, RenderDotC. You wrote a RIB file or called the Ri C API, and any of them would render it. 3Delight was RenderMan-compliant for the better part of two decades and free for non-commercial use since 2001.

How 3Delight came to exist is a story about that spec working exactly as intended, once. Pierre Lachapelle had studied in Montreal alongside Daniel Langlois; the two were on the team behind [Tony de Peltrie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_de_Peltrie), shown at SIGGRAPH in July 1985, a year before Luxo Jr. and three before Tin Toy. It was the first computer-animated human character to carry emotion through facial expression and body movement, i.e. the first time deforming, fleshy surfaces were animated rather than rigid ones. John Lasseter [called it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_de_Peltrie) "the landmark piece, where real, fleshy characters were first animated by computer." Langlois went on to found Softimage. Lachapelle, at about the same time, founded Taarna Studios, named after the heroine of Heavy Metal. Taarna wanted to buy a batch of PRMan seats. Pixar's sales, in the person of Lola Gill, of `renderwoman@pixar.com` fame, would not give a volume rebate. He asked [Dot C Software](https://dotcsw.com/oc.html) the same question about RenderDotC, the other serious compliant renderer at the time, and got the same answer from Cheryl LaMont, Dot C's CEO and the wife of its CTO and RenderDotC's developer, Rick LaMont. So he did the arithmetic: for what either of them wanted, he could hire developers and write his own RenderMan-compliant renderer. He did. It rendered Taarna's IMAX featurette [The Boxer](https://www.isea-symposium-archives.org/art-events/pierre-lachapelle-the-boxer-trailer/), and it was called 3Delight.

The example I could find of somebody using the spec to switch vendors ends with them switching to themselves. That tells you how portable it was in practice.

Today "RenderMan" means Pixar's product. Pixar [consolidated Pro Server and Studio into one "RenderMan"](https://www.fxguide.com/fxfeatured/rendermanris-and-the-start-of-next-25-years/) in 2014. BMRT died in 2000, Pixie in 2009, Aqsis in 2012. 3Delight [is not RenderMan-compliant any more](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3Delight) and says so. The spec is dead as a multi-vendor standard, and the name went to the last implementation standing.

Why it died is worth a paragraph, because NSI is in part an answer to it.

Compliance meant you could switch renderers. In theory. In practice the spec standardised the scene interface and left shading to you: no standard shader library, no production shaders shipped with any compliant renderer. So using a RenderMan renderer, any of them, meant hiring shader writers and TDs to build your look from nothing. Only big facilities could afford that. And once you had built it, you had built it against one renderer's quirks, and you were locked in exactly as hard as if there had never been a spec. Most people using "RenderMan" never understood that the name meant an interface, and their pipelines made sure the distinction never mattered.

Meanwhile Pixar kept adding to the spec. By the time it froze, thirty years of quirks sat on top of Pat Hanrahan's late-1980s design, and not much of that design's economy was still visible.

The interface was portable and the content was not, which made the portability largely theoretical. NSI's answer is OSL, the one shading standard that did become portable, plus a vendor that ships DCC shader translations as OSL files anyone can read. Whether that makes a second NSI renderer genuinely interchangeable remains open. It is at least the right question.

How NSI came about follows directly from this. 3Delight spent years playing catch-up with each new PRMan quirk in the spec, and then stopped, once it was clear who was actually programming against the RenderMan Interface: the renderer developers themselves, writing their own DCC plugins. There were exceptions, and they were short-lived. Affogato, the Softimage exporter I wrote at Rising Sun Pictures, became the base for 3Delight for Softimage, and then Autodesk discontinued Softimage. 3Delight for 3ds Max went the same way. When the only client of your public interface is you, the interface is not buying anybody anything, and you are free to replace it.

And it needed replacing for a reason beyond quirks. RI is a stack machine. `AttributeBegin`, `TransformBegin`, a current transform, a current attribute state, and every primitive inherits whatever is on the stack when it is declared. That was the right call in 1988, for a stream feeding rendering hardware. It is hopeless for interactive editing in 2010, because to change one object you have to reconstruct the stack state that produced it. NSI's [first design rule](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/background.html), "commands are self-contained and do not rely on a current state", is the direct answer to that, and it is why an NSI edit is one call.

Keep that in mind when you read "RenderMan" below. It means `libprman`.

### What Riley is

Riley is RenderMan's scene API, [introduced in RenderMan 22](https://www.fxguide.com/fxfeatured/look-at-renderman-22-and-beyond/) in 2018 as the replacement for the RIB double-buffer path: "a new modern render interface, Riley, which is much faster than the traditional RIB pipeline". It is a C++ abstract class, [`riley::Riley`](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/classRiley.html), with `Create`, `Modify` and `Delete` for each entity type: cameras, geometry prototypes, geometry instances, materials, displacements, light shaders, light instances, integrators, coordinate systems, render outputs, render targets, displays, render views, display filters, sample filters. Plus `SetOptions`, `Render`, `Stop` and `RequestUpdate`.

That list is NSI in C++: push, explicit creation, modification and deletion of retained scene objects, edits between renders, and a render that can stop and restart on request. Two teams who had spent decades writing RIB exporters set out, a few years apart, to replace RIB and landed on the same shape. That is the strongest evidence here that the shape is right.

Handles are typed opaque integers rather than NSI's strings, which is a legitimate design choice and probably the better one for a C++ API. Geometry type is a string, the RI primitive set. Primvars go in an `RtPrimVarList` with explicit detail, which is better than NSI's count inference.

### Why it looks like this

I had assumed, for years, that NSI was passed over at Pixar for the usual reason. In April 2021 Philippe Leprince, then on the RenderMan team at Pixar, and someone I had worked with at DNeg in London in 2007 and 2008, set me straight in a private chat. I quote with the caveat that it was a chat:

"you have no idea how much we needed to cut corners to deliver the first iteration. We would have gladly adopted an external model if possible. We have a whole RATNIH directory with all external dependencies in our repo. Getting a new one approved by legal is a bit painful but we do it on a regular basis."

And then the actual reason:

"In the end the studio requested that we kept the Ri layer more or less intact so they could slowly transition their toolset. At that point our plan changed and we designed something along those lines. Only a handful of engineers were happy about that and the rest knew we would have to do it later anyways."

"so now we have a scenegraph API (used by DCCs) that talks to a low-level renderer API (RIS, XPU, RI, JSON, USD). Not super elegant but it does the job."

This explains the vocabulary section. `Ri:subdivtags` with three ints per tag, `Ri:scheme` as a primvar, the 1989 primitive names and the display-driver pixel path are Ri kept "more or less intact" so a studio with thirty years of RIB tooling could migrate at its own pace. The Scene Graph API exists because Riley alone was not what DCCs wanted to talk to. Its engineers knew that.

Riley is Ri-shaped because Pixar's largest customer is Pixar. That is a perfectly good reason, but it is also why nobody else should expect it to fit them.

The rest is where it falls down.

### What Pixar did with it

**It has been "not yet stable" for eight years.** The RenderMan 24 and [26 Developers' Guides](https://rmanwiki-26.pixar.com/space/REN26/19662207/Developers'+Guide) both say: "Please note the new interactive API (Riley) introduced in RenderMan 22 is not yet stable." The [26 API changes page](https://renderman.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/REN26/pages/19661991/API+Changes+from+25.X+to+26.X): "As of PRMan 26.0, Riley should still be considered an unstable interface" and "The Riley header file version has been incremented to 0.4." The [header itself](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman26/Riley_8h_source.html) opens with: "This is a preview release and is not supported. This API will change substantially in the next release of RenderMan. Names, namespaces, methods, parameters, types, enums, and the structure of the interface itself are expected to change." That paragraph has shipped, unchanged, since 2018.

NSI has been at `NSI_VERSION 2` with eleven functions since the 2018 spec. Riley is at [0.5](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman27/Riley_8h_source.html#l00038) with a disclaimer. Same goal, same design, same year.

**It is write-only, and nobody told you.** NSI's spec [states as a design goal](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/background.html) that data flows one way. Riley's docs say nothing, so the people who tried found out. Gaffer's maintainers, in [their session header](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/src/IECoreRenderMan/Session.h#L57): "Riley is essentially a 'write only' API, so if we want access to any state we need to track it ourselves." Same design decision as NSI. One of them wrote it down.

**It crashes if you call it in the wrong order.** [Same file](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/src/IECoreRenderMan/Session.h#L62): "Options must be provided at construction time, as Riley requires them to be set before any other operations can take place (and indeed, will crash if the Riley instance is destroyed without `SetOptions()` being called)." NSI's `.global` node is an attribute set on a handle like everything else, and you can set it whenever you like.

**It has no serialized form.** There is no way to dump a Riley scene to a file from Riley. RIB export exists, one layer up in the [Scene Graph API](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman26/classScene.html), and RIB is the 1989 interface, not this one. There is a debug trace behind an environment variable, [`RILEY_CAPTURE`](https://rmanwiki-26.pixar.com/space/REN26/152240132/Environment+Variables), producing "python or json", documented in one sentence as "Useful for submitting interactive bugs". Its internal name, per the same page, is "Disgust". I did not make that up.

**Pixar's own bridges do not call it.** RenderMan for Blender, the one bridge with public source, [drives the Scene Graph API](https://github.com/prman-pixar/RenderManForBlender/blob/main/rman_render.py) -- `RixSGManager`, `CreateScene`, `EditBegin`/`EditEnd` -- which sits on top of Riley and adds retained state, edit blocks and RIB export. The [Scene Graph header](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/rman26/RixSceneGraph_8h_source.html) carries the same "preview release and is not supported" language. Pixar needed a second, also-unstable layer to make its API usable for a bridge. NSI exporters call NSI.

**hdPrman is a 2.2 MB adapter between Pixar's public framework and Pixar's own push API.** [hdPrman](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/tree/dev/third_party/renderman/plugin/hdPrman) is 124 `.cpp` files and 66k lines. 84 of those files mention Riley; `riley::` appears 761 times. Its job is to turn Hydra's pull model into Riley's push model, and it takes more code to do that than 3Delight's entire Maya exporter. If you want to know which of Pixar's two interfaces is the real one, follow the arrows: the renderer speaks Riley, and Hydra is translated into it.

**Outside Pixar, I found one user.** [Gaffer](https://github.com/GafferHQ/gaffer/blob/main/src/IECoreRenderMan/Session.h) writes to Riley. It added "preliminary support for rendering with RenderMan" in [1.5.9 in March 2025](https://www.gafferhq.org/documentation/1.5.9.0/ReleaseNotes/1.5.9.0.html) and called it "mature enough for general use" in [1.6.0 in August 2025](https://www.gafferhq.org/documentation/1.6.17.0/ReleaseNotes/1.6.0.0.html). Seven years after Riley shipped. Gaffer is a renderer-agnostic scene framework with Arnold, Cycles and 3Delight back ends, so it is the kind of project that has a reason to write a Riley bridge. I could not find any other public DCC plugin targeting Riley; USD reaches RenderMan through hdPrman.

Compare: four open-source DCC exporters target NSI, plus a Rust workspace, plus a second renderer.

### What Pixar's bridges actually do today

As far as public documentation goes, and Pixar does not document internals, so I will take corrections:

- **Maya.** RenderMan for Maya was [rewritten for 22](https://www.cgchannel.com/2018/09/pixar-ships-renderman-22/) around live rendering to Viewport 2.0. It has its own translation and [renders from memory](https://rmanwiki-26.pixar.com/space/RFM26/21037128/Batch+Rendering+in+Maya) in batch by default, with RIB export kept "to support existing pipelines and farm rendering". It is not built on Hydra; hdPrman in the Maya viewport is a [user-side mayaHydra configuration](https://forum.aousd.org/t/hdprman-for-maya/2549), not RfM.
- **Houdini.** Two paths. The OBJ-context ROP is Pixar's own bridge with RIB export still on the menu. Solaris is pure hdPrman: ["the same RenderMan Hydra Renderer Delegate is what also allows the husk command-line renderer"](https://rmanwiki-26.pixar.com/space/RFH26/19268109/Solaris).
- **Katana.** RenderMan for Katana is a [native Katana render plugin](https://rmanwiki-26.pixar.com/space/RFK26/20217861/RenderMan+26+for+Katana), with live editing of materials and lights. hdPrman is available [in the Katana Viewer](https://renderman.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/RFK24/pages/21430700/hdPrman+in+Katana) with a limitation list that includes "NURBS surfaces cannot be rendered".
- **Blender.** Open source, [on the Scene Graph API](https://github.com/prman-pixar/RenderManForBlender/blob/main/rman_render.py), with RIB dump as an option.

One of four Pixar bridges goes through Hydra for final frames, and only because SideFX built Solaris around it. The other three are direct translators on Pixar's push API, exactly as the 3Delight plugins are on NSI. Pixar's own products make the same choice this post does.

### What RIB became

RIB is not deprecated. It is demoted. [`prman frame.rib`](https://rmanwiki-26.pixar.com/space/REN26/19661967/prman) still works in 26, batch renders from Maya [still spool RIB](https://rmanwiki-26.pixar.com/space/RFM26/21037128/Batch+Rendering+in+Maya) to the farm, and cgchannel's read on 22 was that its use "will presumably be wound down over time". What RenderMan 27 [deprecated in November 2025](https://www.cgchannel.com/2025/11/pixar-releases-renderman-27/) is RIS itself, the CPU integrator, in favour of XPU. Which, per Pixar's own NURBS page, [does not and will not support NURBS](https://rmanwiki-26.pixar.com/space/REN26/19661437/NURBS).

The 1989 file format outlives the 2014 renderer core. Text formats do that.

### Riley in one paragraph

Riley is what NSI would be if you designed it in C++ instead of C, never published a spec, marked it unstable for eight years, kept the write-only nature undocumented, left serialization to the format it was meant to replace, put a second unstable layer on top for your own bridges, and then needed 2.2 MB of adapter to connect it to the framework you told everyone else to use.

The architecture is right. Everything around it is why nobody but Gaffer bothered.

## Case study one: HydraNSI

[HydraNSI](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI) is the useful experiment: a Hydra delegate mapping onto NSI, maintained by NSI's principal designer.

It is a direct measure of the gap between the models.

11,715 lines of C++ plus 745 of OSL. 22 Hydra classes subclassed. 243 NSI calls, using nine of the eleven functions and 14 node types.

Of 8,859 implementation lines, 560 mention NSI: six percent. Eight source files, nearly nine hundred lines, contain no NSI call at all. The three Rprim files ([`mesh.cpp`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/mesh.cpp), [`curves.cpp`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/curves.cpp), [`pointcloud.cpp`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/pointcloud.cpp)) begin with near-identical eighty-line preambles of `GetInitialDirtyBitsMask`, `_PropagateDirtyBits`, `_InitRepr` and `Sync` boilerplate. The point-cloud file is 162 lines and makes two NSI calls.

The comments are the best evidence, and I did not have to hunt for them. On cameras and framing, in [`renderPass.cpp`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/renderPass.cpp#L1070): "If we have an aspect ratio policy from UsdRenderSettings, use that. If not, use the camera's window policy. Can't the latter just be correct? Certainly not. Should all the matching options be named backwards? Certainly so. Does this look designed by two completely separate teams? Hell yes! Hail Hydra." On material outputs, in [`material.cpp`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/material.cpp#L394): "I could not find a way to get the actual value of the material's outputs:surface, etc through Hydra." On lights, in [`light.cpp`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/light.cpp#L237): "Detect Houdini spotlights. They are encoded as USD sphere lights with radius of 0.5 ... We detect them from the presence of the cone angle shaping attribute." On [render tags](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/renderPass.cpp#L792): "I wish we could just iterate over the rprim map in the render index... but there's no access." On [the FPS](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/renderDelegate.cpp#L202), which comes from a Houdini-specific setting: "extremely dodgy but it's the only way I could find."

Two structural observations matter more than the quotes.

First, exposing one native NSI node (`environment`) through Hydra took [a USD schema](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/tree/master/usdNSI), a [`UsdImagingGprimAdapter`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/environmentAdapter.cpp), a custom Rprim type token and an `HdRprim` subclass. The framework's extensibility runs through USD's.

Second, and this one is the real finding: the delegate works because NSI is idempotent. Hydra syncs prims in parallel in an unspecified order, so lights create the attribute nodes their filters will need ["in case the light filter is synced after the light. It is harmless to create it twice"](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/hdNSI/light.cpp#L649), instancers pre-create their prototypes' transforms, and light-linking nodes are created by whichever side gets there first. Create-anything-anytime, connect-by-name, set-at-time is what absorbs Hydra's nondeterminism.

The flat, handle-addressed graph is quietly doing the work the framework was supposed to do.

## Case study two: the direct exporters

[3Delight for Maya](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3DFM) and [3Delight for Houdini](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3delight-for-houdini) are the other half of the experiment: what a DCC integration looks like when it targets NSI directly.

Both have the same shape, and it is a shape you could describe to a junior in five minutes.

A scene walker instantiates one translator object per DCC node type. Each translator has a three-phase lifecycle ([Maya](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3DFM/-/blob/3DFM9.0/src/NSIExportDelegate.h#L30), [Houdini](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3delight-for-houdini/-/blob/master/exporter.h#L26)): create the NSI nodes, set attributes (optionally at several shutter times), connect. The DCC's own identity, a Maya UUID or a Houdini unique ID in IPR and a full path otherwise, is used directly as the NSI handle. DCC dirty callbacks re-run one translator's phases and call `synchronize`. No intermediate scene representation. No change tracker. No adapter layer. The renderer-facing contract is that 169-line header.

3Delight for Maya is 44k lines, of which roughly forty percent is the exporter and the rest is Maya UI, custom shape nodes, viewport fragments and a render view. It makes about four hundred NSI calls. 3Delight for Houdini is 27k lines and makes about three hundred. Shader translation in both is data, not code. Every Maya or Houdini shading node type maps to an `.oso` of the same name, and every attribute of the node is pushed as a shader parameter.

Maya's built-ins are 179 OSL files. Houdini's are 66. The Maya UI for those shaders is generated from OSL metadata.

Adding a shader means shipping a file, not a release.

The cost of this thinness is visible too. When NSI lacked a concept the exporter had to fake it: ["this should really be a `set` but NSI doesn't allow it for now"](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3DFM/-/blob/3DFM9.0/src/NSIExportDefaultLightSet.cpp#L64) in the light-set exporter; per-face assignment [done with shader switches](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3DFM/-/blob/3DFM9.0/src/NSIExportShadingGroup.h#L21) instead of `faceset` nodes; camera-parameter motion blur emitted with `SetAttributeAtTime` ["even though 3Delight doesn't support motion-blurring those attributes, yet."](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3delight-for-houdini/-/blob/master/camera.cpp#L642) And IPR granularity is whatever the exporter bothers to implement. The Houdini plugin [re-exports an entire object](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3delight-for-houdini/-/blob/master/geometry.cpp#L1334) on any SOP change, because it has no diffing layer.

Hydra's change tracker would have handed it one. Credit where it is due.

## Case study three: NSI as the viewport

The claim that Hydra is the interactive one deserves a counterexample. There are four, and they ship.

Each 3Delight plugin runs a live NSI context as an interactive renderer, in the host's viewport where the host allows it, using the same exporter it uses for final frames.

**Maya.** 3Delight for Maya installs a VP2 [`MRenderOverride`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3DFM/-/blob/3DFM9.0/src/dlViewport.h#L255) whose header comment describes it as a "customized Maya VP2 render loop" that will "blend the 3Delight result with Maya VP2 result together, and display the interactive result in viewport". The operation chain is: a user operation that ["updates the color texture from internal 3Delight display driver asynchronously"](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3DFM/-/blob/3DFM9.0/src/dlViewport.h#L62), a Maya scene render restricted to pre-scene UI items, a quad blend, a Maya scene render of post-scene UI items with the grid and image planes excluded and no clear, then HUD and present. So the path-traced image is the shaded pass and Maya's own wireframes, manipulators and selection draw over it. The exporter behind it is [`NSIViewportExport`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3DFM/-/blob/3DFM9.0/src/dlViewport.h#L470), a subclass of the same `NSIExport` that does batch rendering, differing, per its own comment, in that it "has a special way about camera": one screen, output layer and output driver per panel, and a camera or resolution change is a re-export of that screen [followed by `synchronize`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3DFM/-/blob/3DFM9.0/src/dlViewport.cpp#L1720).

**Houdini.** 3Delight for Houdini registers a [`DM_SceneRenderHook`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3delight-for-houdini/-/blob/master/viewport_hook.cpp#L479). It creates a camera, screen, output layer and an output driver named [`3dfh_viewport`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3delight-for-houdini/-/blob/master/viewport_hook.cpp#L23), and passes the address of a shared `hook_image_buffer` through the `outputdriver` node so that the display driver writes buckets straight into memory the hook draws from, without a mutex per bucket. The hook refreshes at [twenty frames per second](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3delight-for-houdini/-/blob/master/viewport_hook.cpp#L27) and draws the buffer as a premultiplied-alpha raster texture with the depth test disabled, fitted to the viewport with margins. The comments are the interesting part. On the camera, [at the draw call](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3delight-for-houdini/-/blob/master/viewport_hook.cpp#L598): "Update NSI camera at each refresh, since we don't get notified when it changes. If its attributes haven't changed since the last update, it will be ignored by 3Delight and the render won't be restarted." On [edit cost](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3delight-for-houdini/-/blob/master/viewport_hook.cpp#L950): "we avoid sending redundant updates to 3Delight. Sending them would still give the correct image without restarting the render, but it would still incur a small performance penalty which, unfortunately, becomes non-negligeable at ~20 updates per second." The cache being described there is two member variables. Last camera transform, last camera parameters.

That is the complete change-tracking layer.

**Cinema 4D.** The [interactive render manager](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3delight-for-cinema-4d/-/blob/master/3Delight/source/InteractiveRenderManager.cpp) is 92 lines. On Cinema 4D's [`EVMSG_CHANGE`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3delight-for-cinema-4d/-/blob/master/3Delight/source/InteractiveRenderManager.cpp#L48) core message it calls the scene parser's `InteractiveUpdate()` and then `synchronize`. On start it constructs the same `SceneParser` the batch path uses, sets its mode to IPR, calls `InitScene` and issues `start` with `interactive` set.

92 lines. That is the complete IPR implementation for that DCC.

**Katana.** Katana's model is the Monitor rather than the viewport, and its Render API delivers live updates as attribute groups. 3Delight for Katana's [`queueDataUpdates`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3DelightForKatana/-/blob/master/src/Renderer.cpp#L396) sorts the incoming updates by a priority order, calls one `ExportLiveUpdateItem` per update, and [`applyPendingDataUpdates`](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3DelightForKatana/-/blob/master/src/Renderer.cpp#L443) issues `synchronize`. [The comment](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/3DelightForKatana/-/blob/master/src/Renderer.cpp#L401) on why the two are serialized against each other is a small lesson in what the API guarantees: "NSI does not allow it and even if it did, it could lead to updates being partially applied and the output being nonsense."

Four DCCs. Four hosts with four different notions of a viewport. One interface.

In none of them is there a change tracker, a render index or a task controller between DCC and renderer. There is the DCC's own change notification, a translator that knows which NSI attributes a change touches, and `synchronize`.

3Delight is tuned for time to first pixel after a `synchronize`, and the API is shaped so a `synchronize` carries only what changed. ["A scene description is a series of edits"](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/background.html) is not a slogan. It is why these four code paths are short.

A mesh modeler of mine, written against the source of these plugins, reproduces the pattern in Rust with a depth AOV so the rasterized gizmos depth-test against the path-traced image. About ten frames per second on simple scenes with the path tracer live, on a twelve-core Xeon of some age. Nobody has heard of it and that is fine -- I mention it only because the pattern transplanted without modification.

## Where NSI is weak

An adversarial review has to cut both ways. NSI has real problems. Fewer than I first listed, and I want to explain why the list shrank, because the reasons are the point.

Most of what looks like an NSI weakness from the outside is one of two things. Either it is a 3Delight implementation detail that the spec never separated from the interface. Or it is a feature Hydra has because USD has it, and USD has it because the RenderMan Interface had it in 1989, and nobody in VFX has used it in production since about 2012.

So, the real ones first.

**The spec does not separate implementation-specific from generic.** This is the actual flaw, and the others below are mostly its shadow. The [`global`](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/nodes/global.html) node reference lists `numberofthreads`, `maximumraydepth.*` and `bucketorder` in the same tables as `license.server`, `networkcache.cachedir` defaulting to `/var/tmp/3DelightCache`, `renderatlowpriority` and `quality.ipr*`. The [`outputdriver`](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/nodes/outputdriver.html) plugin ABI is "implementation specific and not covered by this documentation". Cryptomatte keying, the `zmin`/`zmax` filters and the closure set are 3Delight's.

A second implementer cannot tell from the document which entries it must honour and which it may ignore. `nsi-moonray` found this out the hard way. The spec needs one more column: "required", "optional", "3Delight". That is a documentation fix, not an architectural one, but it is overdue.

**Error handling is a callback with undefined codes.** [No function returns status](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/c-api.html). The error code is ["a numeric identifier ... or 0 when irrelevant"](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/c-api.html). A `SetAttribute` with the wrong type width is dropped with a warning and ["the attribute keeps its previous value"](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/python-api.html). About 28 attributes are typed `double` and 14 `float`, with no discernible principle. `screenwindow` and `crop` sit on the same page and disagree with each other. No words.

**Interpolation is inferred from count.** Whether a primvar is per-face or per-vertex is decided by whether its count matches the face count or the vertex count, with explicit flags ["only strictly needed in rare circumstances"](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/c-api.html). The spec's own tetrahedron example is such a circumstance. Hydra's explicit interpolation tokens are simply better here.

**The document needs an editor.** The [2020 PDF's](https://s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/3delight-downloads/nsi/nsi.pdf) curve sections are literally "??" page references. In the canonical spec. Features 3Delight has shipped since -- `vdbparticles`, MaterialX shader attributes, cryptomatte, `exclusiveshading` -- live in 3Delight's manual rather than in the spec. None of this is architecture. All of it should have been fixed years ago.

**IPR granularity is the exporter's problem.** Hydra tells the renderer which bits of which prim changed. NSI tells it nothing; the exporter decides what to re-send. Good exporters do this well. Lazy ones re-send whole objects. The API neither helps nor hinders. Whether that counts as a weakness depends on whether you think a scene API should contain a change tracker. RenderMan's never did.

Now the ones I struck off, and why.

**"No introspection, no capability negotiation."** True, and irrelevant. A VFX facility evaluates renderers, picks one on what it can do, and then uses that renderer for a decade. Sometimes longer. The renderer does not change under the pipeline from one week to the next, so there is nothing to negotiate at run time. Capability negotiation is an answer to a question that arises in web browsers. Not in a render farm. The spec's ["Extensibility" goal](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/background.html), "as few assumptions as possible built-in about which features the renderer supports", is the correct call for the actual deployment model.

**"The geometry vocabulary is thin."** It is the geometry vocabulary VFX uses. Pixar itself had [converted from NURBS to an all-subdivision pipeline by Toy Story 2](https://www.fxguide.com/fxfeatured/pixars-opensubdiv-v2-a-detailed-look/), in 2000. The rest of the industry followed in the mid-2000s once Maya had subdivs, and by 2012, when OpenSubdiv shipped, polygons and Catmull-Clark subdivision surfaces were simply what production geometry was. For everything. Even for cloth, where Loop has theoretical arguments, most facilities preferred Catmull-Clark. NURBS, quadrics and curve _primitives_ as first-class render geometry are dead in production. Hair is `curves`. Everything else is a mesh.

Only one renderer still renders NURBS without tessellating them first: RenderMan. Arnold and V-Ray accept them and triangulate on load. Hydra's [`UsdImagingNurbsPatchAdapter`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/pxr/usdImaging/usdImaging/nurbsPatchAdapter.cpp) emits a mesh. SideFX [does not write NURBS to USD](https://www.sidefx.com/forum/topic/78028/?page=1) because "it's not really supported in Hydra". Pixar's own NURBS page says [RenderMan XPU "does not and will not support NURBS"](https://rmanwiki-26.pixar.com/space/REN26/19661437/NURBS), while RIS has been [deprecated as of RenderMan 27](https://www.cgchannel.com/2025/11/pixar-releases-renderman-27/). Native NURBS rendering is disappearing at the one place that still had it.

Everyone else tessellates, in the DCC or plugin, and every NSI user can do the same: tessellate, send the mesh. 3Delight dropped native NURBS when it was rewritten as a path tracer because nobody in VFX was sending them. It is adding them back now, with a new node carrying trim curves and stitching to follow, because 3Delight is looking at markets outside VFX where NURBS still matter. The vocabulary follows who is paying, as it should.

And nothing in the spec stops an implementation from accepting `subdivision.scheme "loop"` or `"sqrt3"`. The spec lists what 3Delight does. It does not forbid more.

Hydra's list of prim types is longer because UsdGeom's is, and UsdGeom's is because the RenderMan Interface Specification of 1989 had quadrics, NURBS patches and patch meshes. That is heritage, not requirements analysis.

**"The spec is stale."** Good. A spec that has not needed revision in six years was thought through. The [RenderMan Interface Specification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RenderMan_Interface_Specification) was essentially frozen after subdivision surfaces went in with [3.2 in 2000](https://paulbourke.net/dataformats/rib/RISpec3_2.pdf); 3.2.1 in 2005 was the last revision (and hierarchical subdivision, which 3Delight also implemented and which went nowhere). Nobody called that a weakness; it was the reason you could build a business on it.

What did keep changing in RenderMan-land was shading. RSL grew [shader objects and co-shaders](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/RenderMan_20/rnotes-13.5.html) in Pro Server 13.5, 2007, an absolutely dreadful design. The industry's answer was [OSL](https://www.fxguide.com/quicktakes/sony-releases-open-source-shading-language-osl/), from Sony Imageworks in 2010. OSL is the shading standard now, everywhere -- except RenderMan itself, which went to [C++ Bxdfs in RIS](https://www.fxguide.com/fxfeatured/rendermanris-and-the-start-of-next-25-years/) in 2014, allowed [OSL for pattern nodes only](https://renderman.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/REN24/pages/21759276), and [deprecated RSL and REYES](https://www.cgchannel.com/2016/07/pixar-releases-renderman-21/) in 2016. NSI picked OSL, and picked it before that outcome was obvious.

**"OSL is mandated in everything but name."** Yes. Lights are emission closures, primvars are read with `getattribute()`, AOVs come from `outputvariable()` closures. A renderer without OSL cannot implement NSI faithfully; `nsi-moonray` had to write [an OSL execution DSO](https://github.com/virtualritz/nsi-moonray/tree/master/dso/osl) for MoonRay before NSI shaders rendered as themselves. In 2026 nearly every production renderer runs OSL. This is a requirement, not a flaw.

**"No middleware layer."** Hydra's filtering scene indices are a nice piece of engineering, and I said so above. They are also not the spec's job. The RenderMan spec never had one. PRMan had Ri filter plugins, in C++, PRMan-only, and whoever needed more built it on their side of the API. A company that opens its renderer's interface for anyone to implement is not thereby obliged to also ship everyone's pipeline plumbing.

And NSI does have a filter story, it is just not a framework. NSI has a [Lua binding](https://nsi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/lua-api.html), and an NSI Lua stream parses about as fast as RIB does. A filter is a Lua script that reads one stream and emits another, or runs inside the renderer via `NSIEvaluate`. That is your Ri filter, without the C++, without the plugin ABI, and without being tied to one renderer.

Caveat: the [`nsi-intermediate`](https://github.com/virtualritz/nsi/tree/master/crates/nsi-intermediate) crate is mine, and it is exactly such a layer, built outside the spec. Which is where it belongs.

## Where Hydra is strong

Fine-grained change tracking with parallel sync is serious engineering. A path tracer gets less from it than a rasterizer, but less is not nothing. A single delegate reaches `usdview`, Solaris and `husk`, Katana's viewer, Maya's viewport, Nuke, Blender and Omniverse. In each host, picking, selection, HUD and compositing are already done, so the delegate lands in a finished viewport rather than having to build one. That is what Hydra gives you that NSI does not. It follows from host adoption, not Hydra's architecture. Adoption is out of scope here, but pretending the effect does not exist would be dishonest. The scene index is a good idea, and generative procedurals ([`HdGpGenerativeProcedural`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/pxr/imaging/hdGp/generativeProcedural.h), which read the input scene and emit child prims) are a more powerful model than NSI's opaque `procedural` node. Nested point instancing with per-instance primvars is properly specified. Material networks with per-renderer contexts and MaterialX are handled once for everyone. And [hdPrman](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/tree/dev/third_party/renderman/plugin/hdPrman) proves that a full production renderer fits, at the cost of 2.2 MB of glue and 26 scene-index plugins.

## View one: you own a renderer and need a public API

Three jobs. Expose your scene model. Keep it stable while you change your internals. Let other people build on it, in languages and processes you do not control.

With NSI you write eleven C functions over a node vocabulary that already looks like an offline renderer's scene. Then you are done with the interface layer. Everything else is your renderer.

Your ABI is C, so bindings, RPC wrappers, stream recorders and test harnesses arrive from your users rather than your budget.

Your API is its own file format, so bug reports arrive as `.nsi` files you can render. `nsi-moonray`, the proof that this is not 3Delight-specific, exists and renders, and is the work of a single developer rather than a renderer vendor.

The time it took is an architectural measurement. The [first commit](https://github.com/virtualritz/nsi-moonray/commits/master) is dated 2026-09-05 at 12:40; the most recent, 2026-09-07 at 18:27. In those fifty-four hours, across 90 commits in `nsi-moonray` and about 100 more in the [`nsi-intermediate`](https://github.com/virtualritz/nsi/commits/master/crates/nsi-intermediate) and [`nsi-parse`](https://github.com/virtualritz/nsi/commits/master/crates/nsi-parse) crates it depends on, [MoonRay](https://github.com/OpenMoonRay/moonray) acquired an NSI front end. It [builds a recorded scene straight into a live `scene_rdl2` context](https://github.com/virtualritz/nsi-moonray#readme), with no file or spawned process; streams converging rectangles to the application's output-driver closures; and on `synchronize`, re-sends only what edits touched. It crosses polygon and subdivision meshes with creases and corners, nested and motion-blurred instancing, transform and deformation blur on a scene-wide shutter, perspective, orthographic, fisheye and spherical cameras, built-in, primitive-variable and per-lobe AOVs from NSI output layers, OpenVDB volumes, primitive variables in every NSI interpolation, and lights as emissive geometry. Shading runs OSL: [a MoonRay DSO](https://github.com/virtualritz/nsi-moonray/tree/master/dso/osl) executes the NSI shader network as an OSL group specification, with displacement, `getattribute()` answered from NSI primvars, and 3Delight's shaders rendering unmodified. It also builds as `libnsi_moonray.so`, exporting the eleven C entry points, so an NSI application can load MoonRay where it would load 3Delight. That is roughly 37k lines of Rust and 4.7k of C++.

Two disclosures, because the number above is worthless without them.

Most of that code was written by an LLM, Claude Opus, from a request to add NSI and OSL support to MoonRay, a repository of agent instructions and coding standards, and the spec.

And it is not production software: [the handoff notes](https://github.com/virtualritz/nsi-moonray/blob/master/HANDOFF.md) say the incremental path has not yet been proven incremental, there are two MoonRay crashes [written up for upstream](https://github.com/virtualritz/nsi-moonray/commits/master), and sixty-six tests is not a conformance suite. But the comparison that matters is not with a shipping product. It is with the fact that HydraNSI has been under development [since 2018](https://gitlab.com/3Delight/HydraNSI/-/blob/master/CHANGELOG.md) and [hdMoonray's own documentation](https://docs.openmoonray.org/user-reference/tools/hydra/hdmoonray-houdini/) still lists light filters and render vars as not yet available. That weekend was predicted, and not only by me. In the same April 2021 chat I said I had told Aghiles Kheffache, 3Delight's lead developer since the Taarna days around 1998, that they "need to release a reference implementation of a parser and a lightweight intermediate scene graph representation as OSS to get traction with adoption. Then hooking this up becomes a weekend project." Philippe's reply: "I was about to suggest something similar to Aghiles. low barrier of entry."

3Delight was swamped, and so was Pixar, so neither did. `nsi-parse` and `nsi-intermediate` are that parser and that intermediate representation, five years late, from me instead. `nsi-moonray` is the weekend. Fifty-four hours, to be exact.

The reason a front end goes up in a weekend is the thing this whole post is about. Eleven functions. A node vocabulary that already looks like a renderer's scene. A spec you can read in one sitting.

Or in one context window. That last clause is new in 2026, and it cuts one way.

An interface small enough for a person to hold in their head is now also small enough for a machine to implement over a weekend. 221 headers, 86 schemas and an interface version of 105 is not.

With Hydra you subclass ten to fourteen classes, override eighty to a hundred virtuals, and re-implement the sync-and-commit choreography that every delegate has re-implemented. You link against a specific OpenUSD, and against a specific host's OpenUSD, and ship a binary per host per release. You inherit USD's ontology for lights, primvars and materials whether or not your renderer thinks that way, and hard-code the remapping. You get render settings through whichever of three channels your host uses. You write your own output path for anything that is not a framebuffer. You budget for a rebuild every quarter, plus a rewrite for when Hydra 2.0's renderer interface stops being a stub.

Your reward: hosts that speak Hydra can drive you. Which hosts, and for what, is the next section.

You can do both. I would.

HydraNSI is the existence proof. Put the eleven functions underneath, the delegate on top, and the delegate is a 12k-line adapter somebody else could maintain.

## View two: you write a DCC integration for a renderer

Here the case is genuinely less one-sided, and I want to give Hydra its due before taking it away.

The value proposition for a DCC vendor is real. Write one scene delegate, and every renderer with a Hydra delegate works in your viewport. [Autodesk](https://github.com/Autodesk/maya-hydra), [Foundry](https://learn.foundry.com/katana/Content/ug/using_hydra_viewer/hydra-render-delegates.html), [Blender](https://projects.blender.org/blender/blender/src/branch/main/source/blender/io/usd/hydra) and Nuke all did exactly that. It is why Hydra viewports are everywhere.

But the question here is final-frame rendering of native, non-USD scenes. There the survey is short.

As of September 2026:

- **Houdini** has [no Hydra path except Solaris](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/hdk/_h_d_k__u_s_d_hydra.html). The `/out` [Karma ROP](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/houdini/nodes/out/karma.html) converts OBJ data into an in-memory USD stage and images that. Every third-party delegate sees Houdini only through a `UsdStage`. Arnold, Redshift and V-Ray all keep a classic ROP alongside their Hydra delegate. Houdini 21 switching `husk` to Hydra 2.0 [broke Redshift until a rewrite](https://support.maxon.net/hc/en-us/articles/23795699275548-Redshift-Compatibility-Notice-Hydra-2-0-in-Houdini-21).
- **Katana** feeds its Hydra viewer from the Katana scene graph without USD, but Foundry [positions render delegates strictly as preview](https://learn.foundry.com/katana/Content/ug/using_hydra_viewer/hydra-render-delegates.html). Final frames, live render and AOVs go through the [Katana Render API](https://learn.foundry.com/katana/dev-guide/Plugins/Renderer/RenderAPI.html). RenderMan, Arnold and 3Delight for Katana are all Render API plugins.
- **Cinema 4D** has no Hydra support at all. Redshift, Arnold, Corona, V-Ray and 3Delight are direct SDK plugins.
- **Maya** is the interesting one. [`mayaHydra`](https://github.com/Autodesk/maya-hydra) feeds Hydra from native Maya data through a scene-index chain, and since June 2026 ([v0.8.2](https://github.com/Autodesk/maya-hydra/blob/dev/doc/CHANGELOG.md), [Maya 2027.2 and later](https://github.com/Autodesk/maya-hydra/releases/tag/v0.8.2)) it can drive a delegate from `mayabatch`. Caveats from [its own documentation](https://github.com/Autodesk/maya-hydra/blob/dev/doc/batchRenderSettings.md): render settings come either from USD render settings prims authored in a stage in the Maya scene or "the render delegate itself owns the render settings logic"; "If neither condition is met, batch rendering fails with an error." Maya shading networks translate to `UsdPreviewSurface` or MaterialX, ["only direct texture inputs are supported"](https://github.com/Autodesk/maya-hydra#readme), no Hypershade procedurals. Bifrost, nParticles and fluids are unsupported. Crop regions are a [known issue](https://github.com/Autodesk/maya-hydra/releases/tag/v0.8.2). Chaos's public position is that Maya's Hydra ["needs to mature before we're ready to implement Hydra support"](https://80.lv/articles/a-guide-to-usd-and-hydra-in-v-ray). No major vendor ships a Maya integration whose final-frame path is Hydra.
- **Blender** is the only DCC with an official [native-to-Hydra final-frame path](https://projects.blender.org/blender/blender/src/branch/main/source/blender/io/usd/hydra), and [it is positioned as preview-grade](https://developer.blender.org/docs/release_notes/4.0/add_ons/). Materials go through [MaterialX](https://projects.blender.org/blender/blender/pulls/111765), so most Cycles node trees do not survive.

Is there a route to Hydra with USD-like coverage that does not go through USD? No.

Every native-scene Hydra integration still links the `pxr` libraries -- [`hd` itself depends on `sdf` and `sdr`](https://github.com/PixarAnimationStudios/OpenUSD/blob/dev/pxr/imaging/hd/CMakeLists.txt) -- still presents USD-shaped prims and Sdr-identified materials, and outside Blender and a three-month-old Maya tech preview, none of them render final frames at all.

"Write a delegate and get Maya, Houdini, Katana and C4D for free" is false. Sorry.

What you do get is a slot in the Hydra viewport that Maya, Katana, Solaris and Nuke already built, with the host's picking and compositing wired up for you. That is a real convenience and I am not going to sneer at it.

That is not an architectural property of Hydra; it is the host having chosen Hydra. That is adoption. And NSI is not incapable of it -- see the four plugins above.

Against that, the [3Delight plugins](https://gitlab.com/3Delight) for Maya, Houdini, Katana and Cinema 4D are Apache-2.0, cover final frames, IPR, AOVs, light linking, motion blur, instancing, procedurals and native-shading-network translation, and target eleven C functions that can be implemented by any renderer. They are not small. The exporter proper is twenty-odd thousand lines per DCC.

And that cost is exactly what Hydra promised to amortise across vendors. For final frames, outside Solaris, it has not.

Renderer speaks NSI: you write a translator per node type with a three-phase lifecycle, hook the DCC's dirty callbacks to re-run it, call `synchronize`. Final frames and a live viewport out of the same code.

Renderer speaks Hydra and the DCC is not Solaris: you write a scene delegate or a scene index. 54 virtuals, or a data-source tree, plus USD conventions for every primvar, light and material. Then you write the render-settings, AOV, output and batch machinery yourself, because Hydra does not have them.

And you still end up viewport-only in three of the four DCCs above.

## Verdict

Hydra is a good viewport architecture later declared an offline-rendering interface. Its pull model, framebuffer output, late and fragmented render settings, USD-shaped vocabulary, C++ ABI and quarterly churn follow from its original job. None is incompetence. They are just the wrong answers to a question Hydra was not asked in 2015. Offline delegates pay for that in glue: 2.2 MB for hdPrman; 12k lines of HydraNSI to say what 243 NSI calls say.

NSI is a small, stable, C, push, self-serialising interface designed by people who had already written the exporters it serves. Its weaknesses are those of a small, single-vendor spec: it does not distinguish 3Delight-specific attributes from NSI ones, its typing is sloppy, error reporting is a callback, and its PDF has "??" where the curves chapter should be. Those are documentation and specification problems. Hydra's are inseparable from what Hydra is.

Riley is the control group. Pixar built essentially the NSI architecture for itself, in C++, then did not do the work that would make it broadly usable. It supports the architecture and damns the execution.

For a renderer's public API, NSI is the better architecture by a wide margin; you can always put Hydra on top of it later. For a DCC integration, NSI works for final frames and interactive rendering alike because it does not distinguish between them: a viewport takes small edits and a batch render one large edit. Hydra gives the integrator a place in viewports hosts already built. Useful, but not architecture.
